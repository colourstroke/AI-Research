01 · Start with a distinction

Is electromagnetic radiation the same as light?

Electromagnetic radiation is energy propagated through oscillating electric and magnetic fields.

Visible light is the portion of the electromagnetic spectrum detectable by the human eye.

Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all electromagnetic radiation.

03 · Photons and momentum

How can a massless photon have momentum?

The familiar equation E = mc² describes the rest energy of an object.

For moving particles, use the relativistic energy–momentum relation:

E² = (pc)² + (m₀c²)²

For a photon, its rest mass m₀ = 0.

E = pc
p = E/c = h/λ

A photon has zero rest mass, but it carries energy and momentum.

Terminology: use “zero rest mass,” not “no energy” or “no momentum.” A photon is not matter in the everyday, massive-object sense.

04 · Light meets matter

What happens when a photon encounters a molecule?

A molecule can absorb a photon when the photon energy matches an allowed energy difference between molecular states.

ΔE = hν = hc/λ

The molecule moves from a lower-energy state to an excited state.

05 · Why wavelength matters

Why do molecules absorb specific wavelengths?

Molecules have quantized electronic, vibrational and rotational energy states.

Different transitions require different energy differences. Therefore, absorption depends on photon frequency or wavelength.

A molecule's spectrum can act as a physical fingerprint.

07 · Beer–Lambert law

How does absorbance depend on concentration?

For a suitable absorbing species under defined conditions, absorbance increases with both concentration and optical path length.

A = εcl

08 · Put the law to work

A simple Beer–Lambert example

Suppose a sample has:

  • ε = 100 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹
  • c = 0.002 mol L⁻¹
  • l = 1 cm
A = 100 × 0.002 × 1 = 0.20

What does A = 0.20 mean?

I/I₀ = 10⁻ᴬ
I/I₀ ≈ 0.63

About 63% of the incident intensity is transmitted at that wavelength in this idealized example.

The law has limits: it works best for appropriate dilute solutions and controlled measurement conditions. Scattering, chemical changes, stray light and instrument effects can cause deviations.

09 · From laboratory physics to the atmosphere

Can we use light to investigate invisible atmospheric chemistry?

In atmospheric spectroscopy, sunlight or moonlight can pass through a long atmospheric path before reaching a detector.

Molecules along that path absorb selected wavelengths, leaving spectral features in the received light.

By analysing those features, scientists can infer the presence or abundance of particular atmospheric species, subject to calibration and modelling.
